# What is a Proposition?

- **A proposition** is something that may be **asserted or denied**. It expresses a claim that something **is** or **is not** the case.
- Because a proposition makes a claim about reality, it must have a **truth value**.
- Every proposition is therefore either:
  - True
  - False
- Even if we do not know whether it is true or false, the proposition still has a truth value.
- **Example**
  Water boils at 100°C.
  This proposition expresses a claim about the world that can be **true** or **false**.

# Truth Value of Propositions

- A key feature of propositions is that they possess a **truth** **value**.
- Truth value refers to the logical status of a proposition as being **true** **or false**.
- Importantly, we do not need to know the truth of a proposition for it to have a truth value.
- **Example**
  "There is intelligent life on another planet."
- We may not know whether this claim is true or false, but the proposition **still** **has a truth value**.

# Sentences that are NOT Propositions

- Not every sentence expresses a proposition.
- Only sentences that **assert** **something** capable of being **true** **or false** count as propositions.
- The following types of sentences do **not** **express** **propositions**:
  - Questions
  - Commands
  - Suggestions
  - Proposals
  - Exclamations

# Types of Propositions

- ▶ In logic we study two main kinds of propositions:
  1. **Simple Propositions**
  2. **Compound Propositions**

- ▶ **Simple Proposition**
  A **simple proposition** expresses a single claim and does not contain another proposition within it.

- ▶ Example:
  Socrates is a man.

# Examples of Simple Propositions

> Examples of simple propositions include:
>
- The Earth revolves around the Sun.
- Ali passed the examination.
- Snow is white.
- The library is open today.

> Each of these expresses **a single independent claim** about reality.

# Compound Propositions

- A **compound proposition** contains two or more propositions connected together.
- These propositions are joined using **logical connectives**.
- Common forms of compound propositions include:
  - Negation
  - Conjunction
  - Disjunction
  - Conditional

# Negation

- A **negation** denies a proposition.
- Negation is usually expressed using the word **not**.
- **Example**
  - Original proposition: Socrates is wise.
- Negation
  - Socrates is **not** wise.
- The negation reverses the truth value of the original claim.

# Conjunction

- **A conjunction** combines two propositions using the word and.
- Each component proposition is called a **conunct**.
- **Example**
  Smith is neat **and** Smith is polite.
- This compound proposition contains two claims:
  - Smith is neat
  - Smith is polite
- The conjunction is true only when **both propositions are true**.

# Disjunction

- A **disjunction** connects two propositions using the word or.
- **Example**
  The prisoner has a red hat **or** the prisoner has a white hat.
- Another example:
  Ali will study tonight **or** he will fail the exam.
- Disjunction presents **alternative possibilities**.

# Conditional Propositions

- **A conditional proposition** has the structure **if–then**.
- It expresses a relationship between two propositions.
- **Example**
  If the train is late, then we will miss our appointment.
- This contains two parts:
- > Antecedent
  If the train is late
- > Consequent
  Then we will miss our appointment

# From Propositions to Arguments

- ▶ Single propositions by themselves do not constitute reasoning.
- ▶ Reasoning begins when **multiple propositions** are connected in such a way that one provides support for another.
- ▶ This structure is called an **argument**

# What is an Argument?

> ▶ **argument** is a group of statements or propositions in which:
- one or more statements provide **reasons**
- another statement is supported as the **conclusion**

# The Core Structure of an Argument

- ▶ Every argument has three essential elements:
- ▶ **1. Premises:** Statements that provide reasons or evidence
- ▶ **2. Conclusion:** The statement that is supported
- ▶ **3. Inference:** The reasoning process that connects premises to conclusion
- ▶ Premises → Inference → Conclusion

# Example of an Argument

- ▶ Premise
  All crimes are violations of the law.

- ▶ Premise
  Theft is a crime.

- ▶ Conclusion
  Therefore theft is a violation of the law.

- ▶ The premises provide **logical support** for the conclusion.

# What is a Premise?

- ▶ A premise is a statement that provides a reason for accepting a conclusion.
- ▶ Premises may take different forms:
  - General rule, Observation, Fact, Assumption
- ▶ Example:
  - He must be tired because he worked all night.
  - Premise: He worked all night
  - Conclusion: He is tired

# Premise Indicators

- Certain words signal that a statement functions as a **premise**.
- Common premise indicators include:
  since, because, for, given that, seeing that, inasmuch as for the reason that, owing to the fact that
- **Example**
  - Ali must be at home **because** his car is outside.
  - Premise
    His car is outside.
  - Conclusion
    Ali must be at home.

# What is a Conclusion?

- ▶ The conclusion is the statement that the argument is trying to establish or prove.
- ▶ It is not just another sentence—it is the main point of the argument.
- ▶ Example:
  All humans are mortal.
  Socrates is human.
  Therefore, Socrates is mortal. (Conclusion)

# Conclusion Indicators

- ▶ Certain words indicate that a statement functions as a **conclusion**.
- ▶ Common conclusion indicators include:
  - Therefore, thus, hence, so, accordingly, consequently, it follows that, we may conclude, we may infer
- ▶ **Example**
- ▶ The road is wet.
  **Therefore** it rained last night.

# What is Inference?

- Inference is the process of reasoning that connects premises to a conclusion.
- It is not always explicitly stated—it is something the mind performs.
- Example:
  - If the lights are on, someone is home.
  - The lights are on.
  - Therefore, someone is home.
- Here, inference is the logical movement from premises to conclusion.

# Argument vs Non-Argument

- ▶ Not every group of statements forms an argument.
- ▶ An argument must involve a claim of support.
- ▶ Non-Argument (Description):
  The room is large.
  It has two windows.
- ▶ No reasoning—just information.
- ▶ Argument:
  The room has two windows.
  Therefore, it receives a lot of light.
- ▶ Now there is reasoning.
