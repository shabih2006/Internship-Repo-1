## INTRODUCTION

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is defined by signs and symptoms of an acute infection of the pulmonary parenchyma in an individual who acquired the infection in the community, as distinguished from hospital-acquired (nosocomial) pneumonia (<u>table 1</u>) [1,2]. CAP is a common and potentially serious illness with considerable morbidity. Mild pneumonia is sometimes referred to as "walking pneumonia" because the symptoms do not interfere with normal activity.

The clinical features and diagnosis of CAP in children will be reviewed here. The epidemiology, pathogenesis, and treatment of pneumonia in children are discussed separately.

## **Clinical course**

- **Typical and atypical bacterial pneumonia** – Children who are appropriately treated for CAP generally show signs of improvement within 48 to 72 hours and continue to gradually improve with time. They may continue to have a residual cough for several weeks and have moderate dyspnea on exertion for two to three months.

- **Viral pneumonia** – Children with viral pneumonia generally show signs of improvement within three to seven days, with full resolution of their symptoms by one month. Rarely, the cough may last for several weeks depending on the causative agent (eg, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 [SARS-CoV-2]).

- **Respiratory distress** – Signs of respiratory distress include tachypnea, hypoxemia (peripheral arterial oxygen saturation [SpO<sub>2</sub>] <90 percent on room air), increased work of breathing (intercostal, subcostal, or suprasternal retractions; nasal flaring; grunting; use of accessory muscles), apnea, and altered mental status [1]. Hypoxemia is a sign of severe disease and an indication for hospital admission [<u>1,2</u>]. The presence of grunting has been associated with severe disease and impending respiratory failure [20].

Overall, signs of respiratory distress are more specific than fever or cough for a diagnosis of pneumonia but less sensitive [4]. Children with pneumonia often have tachypnea, but tachypnea is less predictive of radiographically confirmed pneumonia than other signs of respiratory distress, especially early in the course of illness (eg, less than three days) [<u>4,21</u>]. On the other hand, lack of tachypnea may be helpful in excluding pneumonia. In a systematic review of studies evaluating the probability of radiographically confirmed pneumonia in children based on clinical examination findings, the probability of pneumonia was lower if the respiratory rate was ≤40 breaths per minute (likelihood ratio [LR] 0.41, 95% CI 0.17-0.99) [4].

Signs of respiratory distress that are more predictive of pneumonia include retractions, head bobbing, nasal flaring, and hypoxemia (defined differently in different studies, usually SpO<sub>2</sub> <94 to 96 percent in room air) [<u>4,19,22,23</u>]. However, the absence of these findings does not exclude pneumonia. In a systematic review of studies evaluating the probability of radiographically confirmed pneumonia based on clinical examination findings, the probability of pneumonia was higher if signs of increased work of breathing (eg, retractions, nasal flaring, grunting) were present (LRs 1.9-2.7, 95% CI 1.2-5.1) [4]. The absence of these findings had minimal impact on the probability of pneumonia, which was not statistically significant.

If the diagnosis remains uncertain, necrotizing pneumonia can be confirmed by Doppler ultrasonography, chest radiograph (which demonstrates a radiolucent lesion) (image 1), or contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) (image 3) [52,53]. - **Lung abscess** – Lung abscess should be suspected when consolidation is unusually persistent, when pneumonia remains persistently round or masslike, or when the volume of the involved lobe is increased (as suggested by a bulging fissure) [55,56]. Clinical manifestations of lung abscess are nonspecific and similar to those of pneumonia [55], and the course may be indolent. Manifestations include fever, cough, dyspnea, chest pain, anorexia, hemoptysis, and putrid breath [40,57-59], and they are often accompanied by a parapneumonic effusion [39,60,61]. The average duration of fever is four to eight days.

A lung abscess is composed of an accumulation of inflammatory cells accompanied by tissue destruction or necrosis that produces one or more cavities in the lung [55]. Abscess formation may result from inadequate or delayed treatment of lobar pneumonia or, more commonly, develops one to two weeks following an aspiration event. Other predisposing factors include airway obstruction and a congenitally abnormal lung [55]. Anaerobic flora of the upper respiratory tract and S. aureus are the organisms most frequently involved [36].

The diagnosis of lung abscess is suggested by a chest radiograph demonstrating a thick-walled cavity with an air-fluid level (image 1) [55]. Contrast-enhanced CT confirms the diagnosis in questionable clinical situations or if confirmation of the diagnosis is necessary before invasive interventions are performed (eg, percutaneous aspiration, placement of a drainage catheter) [52]. Interventional radiology may be helpful in obtaining a specimen from the abscess cavity for diagnostic studies.

Eighty to 90 percent of lung abscesses in children resolve with antibiotic therapy alone and spontaneous drainage through the tracheobronchial tree, provided that any bronchial obstruction has resolved or, in the case of a foreign body, been removed [57].

**Indications for additional diagnostic testing** — Chest imaging is indicated for children admitted to the hospital to confirm the diagnosis, assess for complications, and document the size and character of parenchymal infiltrates. **Chest imaging** — Chest imaging typically consists of radiographs. Bedside lung ultrasonography has been used to detect lung consolidation in pediatric emergency and inpatient settings [71-75]. - **Views** – Radiographic views depend upon the age of the child [77]. In children older than four years, the frontal posteroanterior (PA) upright chest view is usually obtained to minimize the size of the cardiac shadow [78]. In younger children, the anteroposterior (AP) supine view is preferred because the child's position does not affect the size of the cardiothoracic shadow in this age group and immobilization is easier, improving the likelihood of an adequate inspiration [78].

There is a lack of consensus regarding the need for lateral views to demonstrate infiltrates that may be obscured by the dome of the diaphragm or the cardiac shadow on AP or PA views [<u>1,2,55,79</u>]. In a review of chest radiographs in 201 children with pneumonia, the lateral film was abnormal in 91 percent of 109 children with definite pneumonia [80]. However, the AP or PA views also demonstrated pneumonia in all but three cases. A lateral view may be more valuable in settings where the radiographs are interpreted by nonradiologists.

Of note, the Pediatric Infectious Diseases Society and Infectious Diseases Society of America suggest PA and lateral views for all children who are hospitalized for management of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) [1].

A lateral decubitus view (with the affected side down) may be needed to identify a pleural effusion. (See <u>"Epidemiology, clinical presentation, and evaluation of parapneumonic effusion and empyema in children"</u>, section on '<u>Radiologic evaluation</u>'.))

## **Microbiologic testing** — Our approach to microbiologic testing is generally consistent with that of the Pediatric Infectious Diseases Society and the Infectious Diseases Society of America clinical practice guidelines [<u>1</u>]. (See '<u>Society guideline links</u>' below.)

## **Blood cultures** — We obtain blood cultures in children who require hospitalization because they are more likely to have bacteremia than those who are treated in the outpatient setting. (See "<u>Detection of bacteremia: Blood cultures and other diagnostic tests</u>".)

Observational studies conducted in the postpneumococcal conjugate vaccine era demonstrate prevalences of bacteremic pneumonia ranging from 2.2 to 7 percent in hospitalized children who had blood cultures obtained [<u>104-106</u>]. In a prospective study, blood cultures were obtained in 91 percent of 2358 children who were hospitalized with radiographically confirmed pneumonia and were positive in 2.2 percent (95% CI 1.6-2.9 percent) [<u>106</u>]. Most isolates were susceptible to penicillin. *S. pneumoniae* (46 percent), *S. aureus* (6 percent), and *S. pyogenes* (9 percent) were the most frequently isolated pathogens. In this and other observational studies, the prevalence of bacteremia was increased in patients with complicated pneumonia or pneumonia-related metastatic complications (eg, osteomyelitis) [<u>104-110</u>].

Targeting blood cultures to children who are at increased risk for bacteremia (eg, those with pleural effusion/empyema, admission to the intensive care unit, or immunosuppression) may be clinically effective and reduce costs [<u>110,111</u>]. However, determining which children are at risk for bacteremia at the time of admission can be difficult. In one multicenter study, 6 of the 26 patients with bacteremia were initially thought to have uncomplicated pneumonia [<u>104</u>].

## **Sputum Gram stain and culture** — We obtain sputum samples for Gram stain and culture in children who require hospital admission if they are able to produce sputum [<u>1</u>]. Children younger than five years usually swallow sputum, so it is rarely available for examination.

Although good-quality sputum samples can be obtained by sputum induction [<u>112,113</u>], sputum induction is unpleasant. In addition, in a prospective study of the etiology of pneumonia in hospitalized children <18 years of age, bacterial pathogens were isolated from most induced sputum cultures regardless of sputum quality or whether the child had radiographically confirmed pneumonia, suggesting identification of oropharyngeal carriage rather than the pathogenic etiology of the pneumonia [<u>113</u>].

## **Rapid diagnostic testing** — When available, we prefer rapid diagnostic tests to cultures because the results are available much sooner. Results of rapid diagnostic tests can be helpful in making treatment and/or cohorting decisions for infants and children who are admitted to the hospital with probable CAP, regardless of the suspected etiology [1,114]. Importantly, rapid viral testing cannot be used in isolation to exclude bacterial infection because patients, particularly those who are severely ill, may have a concomitant bacterial-viral infection [115].

Rapid diagnostic testing for respiratory viruses is usually done by real-time (RT) PCR. ## Table 1: Pneumonia terminology

| Term                                               | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Classification by site of acquisition**          |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)                 | An acute infection of the pulmonary parenchyma acquired outside of health care settings                                                                                                                                                     |
| Nosocomial pneumonia                               | An acute infection of the pulmonary parenchyma acquired in hospital settings, which encompasses hospital-acquired pneumonia and ventilator-associated pneumonia                                                                             |
| Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP)                  | Pneumonia acquired ≥48 hours after hospital admission; includes both HAP and VAP                                                                                                                                                            |
| Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)              | Pneumonia acquired ≥48 hours after endotracheal intubation                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| Non-ventilated hospital-acquired pneumonia (nvHAP) | Pneumonia that develops in hospitalized patients who are not on mechanical ventilation nor underwent extubation within 48 hours before pneumonia developed                                                                                  |
| Health care-associated pneumonia (HCAP)            | Retired term, which referred to pneumonia acquired in health care facilities (eg, nursing homes, hemodialysis centers) or after recent hospitalization\*                                                                                    |
| **Classification by etiology**                     |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| Atypical pneumonia                                 | Pneumonia caused by "atypical"‡ pathogens including *Legionella spp*, *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, *Chlamydia pneumoniae*, *Chlamydia psittaci*, *Coxiella burnetii*                                                                            |
| Aspiration pneumonia                               | Pneumonia resulting from entry of gastric or oropharyngeal fluid, which may contain bacteria and/or exogenous substances (eg, ingested food particles or liquids, mineral oil, salt or fresh water) or be of low pH, into the lower airways |
| Chemical pneumonitis                               | Aspiration of substances (eg, acidic gastric fluid) that cause an inflammatory reaction in the lower airways, independent of bacterial infection                                                                                            |
| Bacterial aspiration pneumonia                     | An active infection caused by inoculation of large amounts of bacteria into the lungs via orogastric contents                                                                                                                               |

* The term HCAP was used to identify patients at risk for infection with multidrug-resistant pathogens. This categorization may have been overly sensitive, leading to increased, inappropriately broad antibiotic use.

‡ The origin of the term "atypical" is a matter of debate. The term may refer to the fact that these organisms are not "typical" bacteria (eg, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*), which can be identified by standard microbiologic techniques. Others suggest that atypical refers to the mild nature of the pneumonia caused by some of these organisms compared with pneumonia caused by *S. pneumoniae*.

| Indication                                                            | Comment                                                                                                                                                                                        |
