## INTRODUCTION

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is defined as an acute infection of the pulmonary parenchyma in a patient who has acquired the infection in the community, as distinguished from hospital-acquired (nosocomial) pneumonia. CAP is a common and potentially serious illness with considerable morbidity.

The inpatient treatment of CAP and hospital-acquired pneumonia in children >28 days old will be reviewed here.

Our recommendations are generally consistent with practice guidelines provided by <u>the Pediatric Infectious Diseases Society/Infectious Diseases Society of America</u> and the British Thoracic Society [<u>1,2</u>]. (See 'Society guideline links' below.)

This topic discusses treatment for three categories of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP). These categories are defined as follows:

- **Nonsevere uncomplicated pneumonia** – In this context, nonsevere pneumonia refers to pneumonia that requires hospitalization for one or more indications (eg, age, level of respiratory distress, inability of the caregiver to care for the child at home) (<u>table 1</u>). This category of treatment is only intended for children without pneumonia complications. (See '<u>Nonsevere uncomplicated pneumonia</u>' below.)

- **Severe uncomplicated pneumonia** – Children with severe pneumonia are in moderate to severe distress (eg, nasal flaring, grunting, altered mental status, peripheral oxygen saturation [SpO<sub>2</sub>] <90 percent in room air, capillary refill ≥2 seconds) (<u>table 2</u>). This category of treatment is only intended for children without pneumonia complications. (See '<u>Severe uncomplicated pneumonia</u>' below.)

- **Complicated pneumonia** – Complicated pneumonia refers to pneumonia associated with parapneumonic effusion/empyema, necrotizing pneumonia, or lung abscess. Complicated pneumonia can be associated with either nonsevere or severe clinical manifestations. Complicated pneumonia requires broader antimicrobial therapy than is used for uncomplicated pneumonia. It also may require invasive interventions to drain effusions or remove necrotic tissue. (See '<u>Complicated pneumonia</u>' below and "<u>Management and prognosis of parapneumonic effusion and empyema in children</u>").

# INFECTION CONTROL MEASURES

**Rapid diagnostic tests** — If possible, rapid diagnostic tests should be performed at the time of admission to facilitate patient cohorting and other infection control decisions. We prefer rapid diagnostic tests to cultures because of their general availability, and their results are reported sooner. (See "Community-acquired pneumonia in children: Clinical features and diagnosis", section on 'Rapid diagnostic testing'.)

## General measures

- **Standard precautions** – Standard precautions include hand hygiene and, if exposure to infectious material is anticipated (eg, purulent wound, respiratory secretions), the use of personal protective equipment. Hand hygiene is the single most important measure to prevent the spread of infection. Guidelines for hand hygiene in health care settings can be accessed through the 🔗 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

- **Additional infection control measures** – Additional infection control measures for suspected respiratory pathogens depend on the likely pathogen. However, we favor simplifying the approach by placing all patients with suspected respiratory tract infection on both contact and droplet precautions. If measles or varicella are suspected, airborne precautions are also indicated.

Once the results of rapid diagnostic tests or cultures are available, measures can be narrowed as follows [5,6]:

- Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), parainfluenza, and human metapneumovirus – Contact precautions (ie, gown and gloves) and mask according to standard precautions
- Influenza, rhinovirus, group A *Streptococcus* (GAS; first 24 hours of treatment), and *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* – Droplet precautions (ie, mask within 3 feet)
- Adenovirus – Contact and droplet precautions
- Measles – Airborne precautions (ie, use of fit-tested N95 mask)
- Varicella – Airborne and contact precautions
- Methicillin-susceptible *S. aureus* (MSSA) – Standard precautions
- Methicillin-resistant *S. aureus* (MRSA) or other multidrug-resistant organisms – Contact and droplet precautions if exposed to respiratory secretions and use of dedicated patient equipment

# PRETREATMENT EVALUATION

- **Laboratory evaluation** – The typical laboratory evaluation for children hospitalized with pneumonia includes a complete blood count (CBC) with differential, inflammatory markers, and serum electrolytes.

- **CBC** – When assessed in conjunction with other clinical, laboratory, and radiographic features, the white blood cell (WBC) count can sometimes be helpful in distinguishing between typical bacterial, atypical bacterial, and nonbacterial causes of pneumonia:

- An elevated WBC count (>15,000/microL) is suggestive of a typical bacterial etiology [7]. However, children with *M. pneumoniae*, influenza, or adenovirus pneumonia may also have an elevated WBC count [8-10].

- A normal or reduced WBC count suggests a nonbacterial etiology. However, a severely ill patient with bacterial pneumonia may present with leukopenia with a predominance of immature cells.

- Peripheral eosinophilia is characteristic of afebrile pneumonia of infancy (typically caused by *C. trachomatis*). (See "<u>Chlamydia trachomatis infections in newborns and young infants</u>", section on 'Pneumonia'.)

- **Inflammatory markers** – Baseline inflammatory markers (eg, erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR], C-reactive protein [CRP], serum procalcitonin [PCT]) obtained at admission and followed by serial measurements during treatment may provide useful information about a patient's response to therapy [1,11-16]. They may also assist with clinical management decisions (eg, when to discontinue antimicrobial therapy). (See '<u>Duration of treatment</u>' below.)

Inflammatory marker levels do not reliably differentiate between typical bacterial, atypical bacterial, and viral pneumonia, but they may be helpful for assessing the likelihood of each. In a meta-analysis of observational studies in children, an elevated CRP and PCT performed better than an elevated WBC count in distinguishing bacterial from viral pneumonia; however, the sensitivity and specificity of approximately 70 and 65 percent, respectively, are insufficiently accurate to be used in isolation [17].

In a prospective, multicenter study of 532 children hospitalized with radiographically confirmed community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), median PCT values for typical bacterial etiologies were higher than for atypical bacterial and viral etiologies, but the ranges overlapped [18]. ## **SUPPORTIVE AND ADJUNCTIVE CARE**

**Antipyresis and analgesia** — Fever and pleuritic chest pain should be treated with antipyretics and/or analgesics (eg, <u>acetaminophen</u>, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug [NSAID]). Fever can increase tachypnea and work of breathing in infants and children with pneumonia. Pleuritic chest pain can lead to shallow breathing and impaired ability to cough. Opioid analgesia does not play any role in this setting unless the child has a chest tube in place and experiences breakthrough pain while receiving parenteral <u>ketorolac</u>.

**Respiratory support** — Children hospitalized with pneumonia should receive respiratory support as indicated by their clinical condition [<u>1,2</u>].

For children with hypoxemia, we suggest titrating supplemental oxygen therapy to maintain peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO<sub>2</sub>) ≥92 percent. Other experts use different targets for supplemental oxygen. Gentle bulb suctioning of the nares may help reduce oxygen requirements in infants and children whose nares are blocked with secretions. - **Adjunctive glucocorticoid therapy** – We do not routinely provide glucocorticoid therapy to children hospitalized with pneumonia. Although some data suggest benefit, trials are limited, and more data are needed before this practice can be adopted as routine.

A 2017 systematic review and meta-analysis identified three small pediatric trials assessing the effect of systemic glucocorticoid therapy in children with *M. pneumoniae* pneumonia (two trials) or CAP (one trial) [25]. There were no deaths among the 225 children enrolled in these trials. The time to clinical cure was faster for children assigned to glucocorticoid therapy compared with those in the control group (mean difference 1.6 days, 95% CI 0.6-2.6 days). Children receiving glucocorticoid therapy were also less likely to have early clinical failure (defined as radiographic progression or clinical instability at day 5 through 8) compared with those in the control group (relative risk [RR] 0.41, 95% CI 0.24-0.70). However, the clinical failure rate in the control group was unusually high (66 percent) and not consistent with contemporary practice in resource-abundant settings. Thus, this finding may not be generalizable to other settings.

In a retrospective study of 2244 children treated for CAP in the outpatient setting, 13 percent received adjunctive glucocorticoid therapy, most of whom had underlying asthma [26]. Among children without underlying asthma, use of adjunctive glucocorticoids was associated with increased likelihood of having an unplanned acute care visit for respiratory complaints within 14 days of the CAP diagnosis. This finding suggests that glucocorticoid therapy may have a detrimental effect in children with nonsevere CAP in the absence of underlying asthma.

Additional large, well-designed pediatric trials are needed to clarify the role of adjunctive glucocorticoids in treating CAP.

## ADDITIONAL CONSIDERATIONS

### Duration of treatment

- **Parenteral antibiotic duration** – There are few data to guide decisions about the duration of parenteral therapy for community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) [2,60]. It is common to switch from parenteral antibiotics to oral therapy once the patient is clinically improving, afebrile for 24 to 48 hours, and not vomiting [61].

- **Total antibiotic duration** – The total duration of the antibiotic course is determined by the severity of the pneumonia, the causative agent, and the immune status of the host.

There are few randomized controlled trials available to guide decisions about the appropriate duration of antimicrobial therapy for radiographically confirmed childhood pneumonia that requires hospitalization [2].

- **Uncomplicated CAP** – For children hospitalized with uncomplicated CAP, we generally treat with a five- to seven-day course of combined parenteral and oral therapy.

This is supported by studies demonstrating the effectiveness of shorter courses of therapy for uncomplicated pneumonia [62-64]. In a single-center observational study in children ≥6 months of age who were hospitalized with uncomplicated CAP, rates of treatment failure were similar among those who received antibiotic therapy for 5 to 7 days and 8 to 14 days (3 versus 6 percent; odds ratio [OR] 0.48, 95% CI 0.18-1.20) [62]. Treatment failure was defined as a composite of unanticipated emergency department or outpatient visits, readmission, or death within 30 days after completion of antibiotics.

In a subsequent trial, patients with uncomplicated CAP who were randomly assigned to receive 5 days of antibiotics had a similar response to those assigned to receive 10 days of antibiotics [64]. Additionally, fewer patients who received the shorter course of antibiotics had antibiotic resistance genes detected on end-of-treatment throat swabs.

Randomized trials in adult patients with mild to moderate CAP also suggest that outcomes in patients treated for less than seven days are similar to those in patients treated longer. These trials are discussed separately. (See "Treatment of community-acquired pneumonia in adults in the outpatient setting", section on 'Duration of therapy'.)

- Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) – For samples obtained via bronchoscopy, quantitative culture techniques are suggested to differentiate true infection from upper airway contamination [72-74].

- Guided percutaneous needle aspiration or catheter drainage of a lung abscess – Microbiologic specimens may be obtained by ultrasonography or computed tomography-guided needle aspiration or catheter drainage. Ultrasonography is preferred because of the lack of radiation exposure.

- Lung biopsy, either by a thoracoscopic or thoracotomy approach – Samples obtained by lung biopsy often yield diagnostic information in children who had nondiagnostic BAL [75-77].

# **Discharge criteria** — Discharge criteria for children who have been admitted to the hospital with CAP have not been standardized but typically include [1,52]:

- Vital signs have improved
- Child is able to maintain adequate fluid and nutrition orally
- Improvement in respiratory status has improved
- SpO<sub>2</sub> is ≥90 percent in room air
- Overall clinical status has improved, including level of activity and appetite
- Afebrile or decreased fever for at least 12 to 24 hours
- Mental status is stable and at baseline
- Caregiver is able to administer home antibiotic regimen, and child is able to comply with therapy
- Child can be discharged to a safe and compliant home environment

Outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy (OPAT) is an option for selected patients who require prolonged parenteral treatment (usually for complicated CAP that for some reason cannot be treated with an oral antibiotic) and have stabilized clinically [52,81,82]. Eligibility for OPAT requires a suitable home environment and a pharmacologic agent with a reasonable dosing schedule [83]. Decisions regarding OPAT should involve the caregivers, an infectious diseases specialist (or clinician knowledgeable about the use of antimicrobial agents in OPAT), a hospital pharmacist, and the primary care provider. The services of a visiting nurse may be required for home visits, education of caregiver(s) and observation of caregiver administration, and/or obtaining blood samples for therapeutic monitoring.

- **Prognosis** – Most otherwise healthy children with pneumonia recover without sequelae, even if the pneumonia is complicated [52,79,80,97]. Readmission was more common among children younger than one year of age and children with chronic medical conditions [98].

Although some data suggest that nearly one-half of children who are hospitalized for viral pneumonia have symptoms of asthma five years after hospitalization, it is not clear whether this is related to unrecognized asthma at the time of presentation with pneumonia or a causal relationship between community-acquired viral pneumonia and development of asthma [99,100].

The overall pneumonia mortality rate in resource-abundant countries is <1 per 1000 per year [51,101].
